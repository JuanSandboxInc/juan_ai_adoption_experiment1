Risks of generative AI

As AI becomes more widespread, attention is growing to its risks: individual risks that affect specific people, and systemic risks that affect societies at large.

Individual risks

Bias and fairness

AI systems learn from data produced by people, so they can absorb and repeat its biases, including biases around race, gender, immigration status, socio-economic background and other characteristics.

Data privacy

Data privacy is an individual’s ability to control what information about them is collected, who can see it and what it is used for. Sensitive information entered into an AI tool isn’t always erased afterward.

Information accuracy

AI-generated content can be factually wrong while sounding authoritative. This matters especially in research, journalism, healthcare and law, where a confident error can cause real harm.

Systemic risks

Environmental cost

Training and running large AI models takes significant computing power. The data centres that host these computers use large amounts of energy and water, raising growing concern about the technology’s environmental footprint.

Impact on labour

AI is changing what jobs require, which tasks get automated and how work is valued. The effects are uneven: some fields and types of jobs are affected more than others.

Information pollution

Information pollution is the contamination of the information environment with false, misleading or low-quality content.

Market and power concentration

A small number of companies and countries have an outsized role in developing AI and shaping how people use it.

Bias and fairness

AI systems learn from data produced by people, so they can absorb and repeat its biases, including biases around race, gender, immigration status, socio-economic background and other characteristics. A system trained on biased data will produce biased outputs, often with no obvious sign that it’s happening.

Here are four examples of bias in how AI models are built and used, and how it affects people:

  1. A widely used machine learning algorithm in the American healthcare system predicted patients’ needs from their healthcare spending. Because the US healthcare system spends less on Black patients on average, the algorithm wrongly concluded that Black patients were healthier and needed less support than equally sick white patients.15
  2. The Dutch tax authority used a self-learning risk model to flag childcare benefit claims for possible fraud, with dual nationality as one of its risk indicators. Because immigrant families had been audited disproportionately in the past, the model learned to treat a foreign background as a marker of fraud and contributed to wrongful fraud accusations in a scandal that affected an estimated 26,000 families.16
  3. Amazon built a machine learning tool to score job applicants and trained it on resumes it had received in the past. Because most had come from men, the model learned to prefer male candidates, reproducing the gender imbalance in its training data. Amazon scrapped the tool when it concluded it could not be made reliably fair toward applicants of different genders.17
  4. US police forces receive facial recognition reports to use as investigative leads, but in practice officers have treated software matches as positive identifications. This over-trust in the computer’s output has led to wrongful arrests.18
